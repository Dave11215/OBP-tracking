Running sessions

Easy Run + strides — Zone 2

30–40 min conversational (HR 120–132, soft cap 145), then 4–6 x 10–15s strides. Keep it genuinely easy.

Quality Run — midweek hard (gated)

5K tempo / threshold. Warm up, finish strong but not to failure, cadence high. Swap to Bike VO2 if legs/HRV need it.

Track intervals — speed (gated)

WU 12–15 min + drills, then 5–6 x 400–800 m at ~5K effort (HR to upper Z4, cap ~172) with generous recovery, CD. Never a time trial.

Long Run — weekend endurance

Easy-to-moderate, progressing ~a mile a week (~4 → 5 → 6 → 6–7). Outdoors on the weekend.

Bike sessions

Zone 2 Bike — easy base, joint-sparing

Conversational Zone 2, HR 120–132 (soft cap 145), 45–90 min. Long outdoor ride on the weekend; carbs + salted fluid past ~75 min.

Bike VO2 — impact-free hard

WU 10–12 min, intervals at ~90–95% max HR (3–4 min on / 3–4 easy), CD. Redline is fine, no impact. Never to failure. This is where the over-threshold work lives.

What we track, and why

Track towards
  • Vigorous minutes, chronic (28-day)The dose that matters is chronic, not acute. One hard session does not move it; a season of them does.
  • Vigorous days per weekCountable. Garmin computes it natively over a rolling 6-week window.
  • Strength sessions per weekStrength and aerobic together beat either alone: HR 0.60, vs 0.71 aerobic-only and 0.89 strength-only. The dose optimum is about 40 min/week, so the 2x floor is already right. Do not inflate it. The real risk is letting cardio crowd it out.
  • Resting HR, trendRR 1.09 per 10 bpm across 1.2M people. Better evidenced than HRV. Read the trend, never the level.
  • Sleep durationShort sleep in midlife carries real risk. This is the window where it counts.
Govern by (monitor, do not target)
  • HRV, overnight, raw msA governor, not a goal. It answers "go hard today?" It is not a scoreboard. Use the raw number, not the status label.
  • Sleep continuityAwakenings and restless moments.
  • Acute:chronic load ratioOverreach management, not a health metric.

The short list: what to actually do

  1. Win the winter. Build the November-to-March plan in September. Last winter cost almost 8 bpm of resting HR and five months of buyback; no single workout matters as much as not repeating it.
  2. Never two one-session strength weeks in a row. Both layoffs started that way. If the cause is schedule, fix it that week; if the cause is injury or illness, write the comeback plan the same week.
  3. Lights out by 10:00. Wake time will not move, so sleep is only bought on the front end: each hour later costs about 41 minutes. Run 10:00 pm for two weeks and judge it by total sleep, not by feel.
  4. On hard training days, schedule the midday nap. Pick the time in advance instead of waiting for the crash to pick it.
  5. Protect the night before, not the number in the morning. Sleep predicts next-day readiness about twice as strongly as any heart metric.
  6. Coach from the 28-day line. One morning's reading is too weak to act on, in either direction.
  7. Score the rebalance weekly. Count strength versus cardio sessions every week; the 50/50 decision is only real if the weekly count says so.

Strength layoffs start small, and the warning shows up early

What to learn: Your rule is at least two strength sessions a week. Neither long layoff started with a sudden stop; each started with a run of one-session weeks that then slid to zero, and 16 of the 37 weeks on this strip ended up at zero. July was following the same script, three straight one-session weeks, before the last two weeks got back to two. The practical rule: two one-session weeks in a row is the moment to act. If the cause is schedule, fix the schedule; if the cause is an injury or illness (as it was in the spring), that is the moment to plan what can still be trained and what the return looks like, so the interruption ends as a pause instead of a five-month rebuild.

Sleep is what actually predicts readiness

What to learn: Sleep score correlates with next-morning Training Readiness at 0.47 and sleep duration at 0.35, roughly double anything cardiac (HRV 0.19, resting HR 0.12). Protecting the night before is worth more than watching the wrist. A readiness dip after a short night is expected, not a reason to restructure training.

Bedtime is the only sleep lever, and it is barely used

What to learn: Wake time is pinned near 6:20 regardless of bedtime, so a later night is not slept off in the morning: each hour later to bed costs about 41 minutes of sleep. July sleep duration (about 6.9 h) sits near the yearly low even while every recovery metric is at its best, which means the two systems are separate.
